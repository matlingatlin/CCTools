=== METHOD ===
# Abstention Threshold Design

Decide when a system should stop answering. An abstention threshold is a business
decision disguised as a number: it trades **coverage** (how much you answer) for
**precision** (how right you are when you do). Derive it from the score distribution
against a stated target — never from a round number that felt safe.

## When to use
- Being wrong costs more than being silent, and the model is sometimes wrong.
- Someone proposes "flag anything below 0.8 for review" and nobody can say what 0.8 buys.
- You need a defensible auto-approval rate: "we auto-ship X% at Y% precision."
- A human review queue exists or is being proposed and its intake rule is undefined.
- Quality complaints and "too much manual review" are being reported at the same time.

**When NOT to use:** escalating malformed records in a bulk deterministic parse for cost
reasons (`hybrid-parse-escalation`); proving a judge's scores match human labels
(`llm-judge-calibration`); gating irreversible or outward-facing actions, which are gated
on *consequence* whether or not the model is confident (`agent-blast-radius-guard`).

## Steps

1. **State both numbers BEFORE looking at any score.** (a) the precision/quality target
   on the **answered** segment, and (b) the **minimum acceptable coverage** — the answer
   rate below which the feature stops being worth shipping. Name who agreed to each. A
   target with no coverage floor always resolves to "abstain more," which is how a feature
   quietly becomes a manual process.

   **When you set the target, weigh it against coverage.** A business that names 99% without
   naming what it costs usually has not priced the abstained segment: 95% precision at 90%
   coverage often serves users better than 99% at 40%. Have that argument HERE, in step 1,
   while the target is still open. Once the pair is agreed it is binding — step 6 never trades
   precision away to buy coverage, and a cut that misses the agreed target escalates (step 7)
   rather than quietly settling for a number that "often" suffices.
2. **List candidate uncertainty measures — plural.** Self-consistency spread across k
   samples; a judge or verifier score; retrieval margin (top-1 minus top-2 similarity);
   validator/schema failure count; ensemble or cross-model disagreement; distance from
   the training distribution; the model's own logprob or verbalized confidence. Test 2–3.
3. **Build a labeled holdout.** A few hundred real cases with ground-truth
   correct/incorrect, sampled from actual traffic including the hard slices
   (`eval-set-curation` when sampling is the hard part; `synthetic-eval-data-generation`
   when there is no traffic yet). Never tune the cut on data used to build or prompt the
   system.
4. **Validate that the measure separates right from wrong — this is what makes the rest
   legitimate.** Per candidate: bucket the holdout by score decile and report observed
   accuracy per bucket; compute AUROC (or rank correlation) against the correct/incorrect
   label.
   - **AUROC ≈ 0.5, or flat accuracy across buckets** → the measure carries no
     information. **Do not threshold it.** Return to step 2 with a different signal. A cut
     on an uninformative score abstains on a random subset: full coverage loss, zero
     precision gain.
   - **Clearly above chance and roughly monotone** → usable. Record the number.
5. **Sweep the coverage–risk curve.** Move the threshold across the score range; at each
   point record coverage, precision/error on the answered segment, and absolute abstained
   volume per day. Eight to ten rows is enough. This table, not intuition, is what you
   review with stakeholders.
6. **Cut where the precision target is met on the ANSWERED segment — then check the
   coverage floor.** If a threshold satisfies both, take the one with the highest
   coverage. If none does, say so and choose explicitly: relax a target with the owner on
   the record, improve the model or the signal (`measured-optimization-loop`), or don't
   ship this autonomously. Quietly honoring the precision target and eating the coverage
   loss is the most common failure here.
7. **Design the abstained path, with a named receiver.** For everything below the cut,
   specify who or what handles it (person, team, queue, safer fallback, degraded
   "unverified" answer), its capacity per day against the abstained volume from step 5,
   the latency the user sees, and what the user is shown. A threshold whose overflow has
   no owner has not reduced risk; it moved it somewhere nobody measures.
8. **Re-validate on a cadence and on every change.** A new model version, prompt,
   retrieval index, or traffic mix shifts the score distribution, so the same number means
   something different. Monitor the abstention rate as the drift alarm — it moving under a
   fixed threshold means the distribution moved — and re-run steps 3–6.

## The four traps this exists to prevent

| Trap | Why it looks fine | The counter |
|---|---|---|
| **Thresholding an uncalibrated score.** A model's own confidence (logprob or self-reported 0–1) is often near-constant and near-1.0, including on wrong answers. | It is the score already there, and 0.9 sounds meaningful. | Step 4 is mandatory. Flat buckets or AUROC ≈ 0.5 disqualifies the measure — swap the signal, don't tune the cut. |
| **Abstention treated as free.** 99% precision at 40% coverage is celebrated as a quality win. | The dashboard only shows the answered segment. | The step-1 coverage floor, and quoting precision and coverage as one pair — never precision alone. |
| **Nobody owns the rejects.** The cut ships; the queue fills. | Model metrics improve immediately. | Step 7: named receiver with stated capacity vs measured abstained volume. No receiver, no threshold. |
| **Set once, trusted forever.** The number sits in config and nobody revisits it. | It was derived carefully — once. | Step 8: cadence plus abstention-rate monitoring. A threshold is a calibration, not a constant. |

## Example
**Before:** "Invoice line-item extraction auto-posts when model confidence > 0.9."
**After:** On a 400-invoice holdout, model confidence has AUROC 0.54 (wrong extractions
average 0.93, right ones 0.95) — signal discarded. Self-consistency across 5 samples
scores AUROC 0.81. Sweep: full field agreement → 96% precision at 71% coverage; ≥4/5
agreement → 91% at 88%. Target 95% precision, floor 60% coverage, so full agreement is
the cut. The abstained 29% ≈ 140 invoices/day against an AP team reviewing 200; they land
in the exceptions queue with disagreeing fields highlighted. Re-validated each model upgrade,
alerting if the abstention rate leaves 24–34%.

## Rules
- **No threshold on an unvalidated measure.** Skipping step 4 makes the number decoration;
  report the missing validation instead of producing a cut.
- **Precision and coverage are always quoted together.** A precision figure with no
  coverage beside it is not a result.
- **The abstained path is part of the design, not follow-up work.**
- **Consequence gating is separate and composes with this** — irreversible actions need a
  human gate at any confidence (`agent-blast-radius-guard`).
- Method only: your own reasoning and scoring runs over your own holdout. No external CLI
  installs, no credentials, no hooks.

## In this repo (one instance)
The factory's auto-vs-gate decisions are abstention thresholds. "Human gate ∝ autonomy" is
the coverage–risk tradeoff in prose: description sharpening auto-applies (high coverage,
cheap errors) while merges and deletions abstain to the human-gate ledger under
`pipeline/ledgers/`. When a talent's eval verdict is the uncertainty measure, apply step 4
first — a verdict that reads "passed" for nearly every talent has AUROC near 0.5 against
real quality and cannot carry an auto-adopt threshold. State the intended auto-adopt rate
in `pipeline/BRAIN.md` before choosing the bar, and re-check it whenever scenario design
changes.
